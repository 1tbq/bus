Step 2: Hooks and openings

Your first sentence has one job: make the reader want the second sentence.

Learn

Five ways to hook a reader

ActionDrop the reader into something happening.
"The bus swerved, and my lunch flew across the aisle."
DialogueStart with someone speaking.
"'Don't open the door,' Mum whispered."
Mystery statementSay something strange that needs explaining.
"Nobody in our street had seen Mrs Kowalski for eleven days."
Sensory descriptionPut the reader inside the scene using the senses.
"Rain hammered the tin roof so hard I couldn't think."
QuestionAsk the reader something they can't ignore.
"Have you ever told a lie you couldn't take back?"

Avoid: "Hi, my name is...", "One day...", "It was a normal day...", or waking up and getting ready for school. Start as close to the problem as you can.

Step 3: Show, don't tell

Telling gives the reader the answer. Showing gives them the evidence and lets them work it out.

Learn
TellingNames the feeling or quality directly.
"Mia was nervous."
ShowingUses actions, body language, dialogue and the senses.
"Mia chewed the sleeve of her jumper and checked the door every few seconds."

Tip: If your sentence contains a feeling word like sad, angry, scared, happy, nervous, ask yourself: what would that look like if I filmed it?

But: telling is still useful for skipping time quickly, like "Three weeks passed." Save showing for the moments that matter.

Step 4: Narrative structure

A strong narrative has a shape. Tension builds, peaks, and then something changes.

Learn

The five stages

1. OrientationWho, where, when. Keep it short and get to the problem quickly.
2. ComplicationThe problem that disrupts normal life and starts the story moving.
3. Rising actionThings get harder. Choices, obstacles, tension building.
4. ClimaxThe turning point. The moment of highest tension where something has to give.
5. ResolutionThe aftermath. What has changed, especially in the character?

Common trap: spending half the story on the orientation, then rushing the climax and ending with "and then I woke up." Give the climax the most detail.

Step 5: Character and setting

Readers believe in characters they can see and settings they can feel.

Learn

Reveal character with STEAL

Instead of telling the reader "Jordan was rude", let them steal clues from the character.

S: SpeechWhat they say and how they say it.
T: ThoughtsWhat goes on inside their head.
E: Effect on othersHow other people react to them.
A: ActionsWhat they do, especially when no one is watching.
L: LooksAppearance, clothing, body language.

Build setting with the senses

Most students only describe what a place looks like. Add sound, smell, touch and taste and your setting starts to create mood. A hospital at 3 am and a beach at noon should feel completely different to read.
